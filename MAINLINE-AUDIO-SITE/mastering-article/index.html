Why Mastering Is the Final Step
Most Artists Undervalue

By Henry Still · Mainline Audio · 2025

On mastering
& the final step

In today's music landscape, it's easier than ever to create and release a track. But one area I consistently see misunderstood — and often overlooked — is mastering. Its job isn't to be noticed. It's to make sure nothing gets in the way of the experience.

The misconception

How mastering gets misread

A final luxury — or a necessary step?

Within the production community, mastering is often seen as highly technical — elusive, even mysterious, and sometimes viewed as almost mystical or magical. While there's truth to that, it can also make it feel like a final luxury rather than a necessary step.

Outside of that world, most listeners aren't thinking about mastering at all. In most cases, they're not even thinking about the mix. They care about the artist and how the record feels when it plays.

That's exactly why mastering matters.

What mastering actually does

Translation

The record has to hold up everywhere

Mastering is about translation. A track might sound great in the room it was created in, but that doesn't guarantee it will hold up across streaming platforms, cars, headphones, or club systems.

It ensures consistency, balance, and clarity — and while it can enhance and refine, it's not a replacement for a strong mix.

Alignment

Built on something solid

The best results come from alignment — when artists, producers, and engineers are all working toward the same vision.

That translation only works when the record is already built on something solid. Mastering reveals what's there. It doesn't manufacture what isn't.

The philosophy

Preserving the vision

When it's done right, no one talks about it

What I've learned is that mastering isn't about adding something new — it's about preserving what's already there and making sure it reaches the listener intact.

When it's done right, no one talks about it. They just feel the music the way it was intended.

That's the standard I work to at every session. Not to be heard — but to make sure the record is.

They just feel the music
the way it was intended.
